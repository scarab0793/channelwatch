English Channel

Small boat arrivals

More than 200,000 people have arrived by small boat since 2018. Fewer are coming in 2026, but each boat carries more people: the average is a record of around 67 per boat, and in the week to 30 October it was about 78 (934 people in 12 boats).

Home Office annual figures. 2026: provisional, to 30 October. The 2022 peak was 45,774.

Who arrives

  • 82% of arrivals in 2025/26 were male.
  • Eritrea was the top country of origin in 2025, with more than twice as many arrivals as in 2024.
  • About 40% of asylum claimants arrived by small boat.

A busy September

  • 781 people arrived on 24 September, the busiest day of the year.
  • Two boats landed undetected within two days; the Home Secretary ordered an urgent review.
  • The previous undetected landing was in September 2025.

What changed in 2026

  • From 2 June the statutory duty to provide support became a power: the government may provide it, but no longer must.
  • People found working illegally can lose their support.
  • Claims made after 2 March get 30 months of protection instead of five years.
  • The bill before Parliament would require refugees who later earn enough to repay about £10,000.
  • The government aims to close all asylum hotels by 2029; fewer than 160 remain, against about 400 at the peak.

Context

  • The Migration Observatory finds that, adjusted for age, foreign nationals are slightly under-represented in prison, because migrants are younger on average.
  • For sexual offences, foreign nationals are over-represented among those convicted.
  • Studies find no link between the migrant share of an area and violent crime. One found a 1 percentage point rise in the asylum seeker share was associated with 1.1% more property crime.
  • Most offences never lead to a conviction, so true offending rates are unknown for every group.

Analysis 1 · Country of origin

Why do most arrivals come from Eritrea?

The reasons people leave Eritrea have not changed in decades. What changed in 2024–25 were the routes and the options, and the UK became an especially attractive end point.

7,602Eritrean small boat arrivals in 2025, 19% of the total
×2+increase on 2024
87%initial grant rate in 2024–25 (2021–23: 96–99%)

Why people leave

Eritrea's compulsory national service has no real end date, and many people stay in it for decades. Human Rights Watch calls it forced labour; a UN investigator has reported torture and sexual violence during service, and draft evaders and their families are punished. The UN estimates more than 10,000 people are arbitrarily detained, and there is no independent press. The Mixed Migration Centre reported in 2025 that departures had not fallen significantly and that "the root causes remain unchanged."

What changed in 2024–25

Many Eritreans used to stay in Sudan and Ethiopia. The war in Sudan and conflict in Ethiopia made those refuges and routes dangerous. Eastern Libya banned Eritrean nationals, and smuggling networks became more predatory. Canada paused new private sponsorship registrations for 2024–25, which had kept many from moving on. Eritrean arrivals on the Central Mediterranean fell by almost 90% by 2024, then rose sharply again in early 2025.

Why the UK

The UK grant rate is far above average, and returns are practically impossible: Eritrea stopped issuing the emergency travel documents needed for removals in March 2026. On 15 October the Home Secretary threatened Asmara with a visa ban; Eritrea said it would not negotiate under pressure. More than 35,000 Eritreans have claimed asylum in the UK since 2014, and an established community draws later arrivals. That last point is a general finding of migration research; we found no UK-specific survey on it.

ConclusionUK pressure is likely to have limited effect: as the Migration Observatory notes, people granted asylum cannot be returned anyway. Numbers depend more on the wars south of the Sahara and on resettlement options.

Analysis 2 · The UK economy

What it costs, and what it could return

Asylum costs are small against total public spending but large within the Home Office budget, and they were badly planned for years. The biggest item is not money given to claimants but accommodation.

£4.36bnHome Office asylum spending, 2025–26 (peak: £5.4bn, 2023–24)
×10increase since 2011–12 (then £538m)
76%of accommodation contract costs went on hotels (2024–25)

Where the money goes

Claimants' weekly cash (£49.18, or £9.95 in hotels) is 35% lower in real terms than in 2000. Accommodation drives the cost: a hotel night is about £144 per person, a dispersal flat about £23. In 2024–25 hotels took 76% of accommodation contract costs while housing about a third of claimants. That is why faster decisions and hotel closures cut spending, which has fallen by about £1bn since 2023–24.

Planning failures and the aid budget

The IFS found that between 2021–22 and 2023–24 the Home Office planned to spend £0.3bn and actually spent £7.9bn, covering the gap from the Treasury reserve. Part of the cost falls on the aid budget: in 2023, £4.3bn, or 28% of UK aid, went on supporting refugees in the UK, money taken directly from programmes abroad.

The labour market

Claimants usually cannot work for 12 months, and after that only in shortage occupations. Of recognised refugees, only about 20% were working a year after their decision and roughly half after five years. Women's employment is much lower, and most jobs are low-paid. A 2026 Center for Global Development estimate puts the value of allowing work after six months at about £294m per annual cohort in savings and tax, roughly 3.5 times what the planned £10,000 repayment charge would recover.

ConclusionAsylum is roughly 0.3% of total public spending. The economic debate is therefore about efficiency: how fast decisions are made, where people live while they wait, and when they can start work. Estimates show large savings on those levers and small ones from the revenue of "tougher" measures.
Sources: Migration Observatory · IFS · ICAI · CGD · NAO / LBC · LBC

Analysis 3 · National politics

Falling numbers, high tension

Almost every migration measure fell in 2026, but the issue's political weight did not. In Westminster, migration is where governments lose the most voters.

21%of Britons name immigration as the country's biggest problem, the highest of 107 countries Gallup surveyed
1,453councillors and 14 councils won by Reform UK in May 2026
27 / 22 / 20Labour / Conservative / Reform, YouGov, 25–26 Oct

Reform's rise and the government's answer

Reform UK led the polls for more than a year. YouGov finds 86% of its supporters rank immigration among the top issues, against 42% of the public. In May's local elections Labour lost 1,496 councillors; Keir Starmer resigned in June and Andy Burnham became prime minister in July. Burnham kept Shabana Mahmood as Home Secretary, and the government tightened rather than softened its line: the statutory duty to support was removed, protection was cut to 30 months, a bill with repayment and a narrower definition of family life is before Parliament, and Eritrea was threatened with a visa ban.

Numbers versus mood

The government points to results: 43% fewer small boat arrivals, half as many people in hotels, the smallest backlog since 2019 and more returns. The Conservatives and Reform say it is not enough ("they will never stop the boats"), while rights groups say the measures come at refugees' expense. Gallup's data show the UK's foreign-born share is no higher than Norway's or the Netherlands', yet concern is much stronger. Public mood is shaped by visible signals as well as numbers: hotels, videos of landings and high-profile crimes.

Trust

In May, Reform was most trusted on immigration (38%), ahead of Labour (22%) and the Conservatives (16%). Labour edged ahead in October's YouGov polls, and in the latest one (25–26 October) Reform fell to 20%, its lowest since the tracker restarted in January 2025. That suggests Reform's support is not fully solid: only 29% of its current voters would not consider another party.

ConclusionThe government is trying to win voters back on Reform's own ground, and so far it is partly working. The risk is that each new restriction reinforces the message that migration is the most important issue, and on that issue voters still find Reform the most credible.
Sources: Gallup · YouGov · TB Mag – elections · NPR · ITV · BritPolls

Analysis 4 · Local politics

Decided in London, felt in the village

The Home Office decides where asylum seekers live; councils deal with the consequences. As hotels close, the conflict moves from towns to villages.

0say councils have over which hotels the Home Office uses
≈1,250people in the plans near Piddington and Barnham
<160hotels left, from about 400 at the peak

Limited powers

Accommodation runs on national contracts, and the Home Office chooses hotels and providers. Reform UK won ten councils in 2025 and promised to use "every instrument of power" to stop hotels, but the tools are narrow, mostly planning and change-of-use rules. In Epping, the High Court temporarily barred use of the Bell Hotel in August 2025, but the Court of Appeal overturned the ruling. The hotel finally closed in July 2026 on the Home Office's own decision.

The new front line: military sites and rural properties

The government is replacing hotels with large, basic sites, typically next to small villages: Barnham, Piddington, Linton-on-Ouse (700 residents, up to 1,200 asylum seekers). At Barnham the Home Office has filed an Urgent Crown Development application, so the plan does not depend on a local planning decision. Opposition crosses party lines: West Suffolk, Fenland, Conwy and West Northamptonshire councils all object. Several villages are considering symbolic "independence" referendums with no legal effect.

Policing and community

Local police carry a heavy load. Gosport needed a town-wide dispersal order and saw 12 arrests. Councils often hear about decisions last: Epping and West Northamptonshire both complained publicly. Brighton & Hove's leadership, by contrast, asked for a review because a hotel was closing, a reminder that local views differ.

ConclusionThe local level is where central decisions win or lose legitimacy. Until councils get advance notice and a real say, conflict between villages, police and the Home Office is likely to continue, whichever party runs the council.

Analysis 5 · Sense of safety

What people feel, and what is measured

The sense of safety is shaped by real cases and by misperceptions together. Serious cases do happen, but the public estimates foreign nationals' share of crime at about twice the statistical reality.

25% vs 12%estimated vs actual foreign share of the prison population
30% vs 15%the same for convictions for sexual offences
11%feel unsafe in their own local area

Misperceptions

A 2026 King's College London study found Britons believe most crimes are rising, though since 2010 burglary and vehicle theft have roughly halved and phone theft has fallen by about 70%; shoplifting is the exception. People guess foreign nationals make up 25% of prisoners (actual: 12%) and 30% of those convicted of sexual offences (actual: 15%); Reform voters guess 50%. Those who overestimate are much more likely to back harsher sentences for immigrants.

Real reasons for concern

The fear is not baseless. Foreign nationals are over-represented among those convicted of sexual offences, and serious cases such as the Nuneaton abduction, the Crewe assault or the Hereford attack rightly cause alarm. In many cases the offender was a refused claimant or a convicted person awaiting deportation, and 19,470 such offenders live in the community. These cases often expose failures of the system: removals that never happen, claimants who abscond, prisoners released in error.

Data gaps and rumours

There is no reliable national data on whether crime changes around asylum accommodation. Police forces record incidents without a specific marker. Dorset Police, for example, logged 286 incidents at asylum accommodation addresses in 2025, a figure that includes protests and cases not involving residents. Rumour fills the gap, as with the Gosport "signal jammers". Asylum seekers' own safety is part of the picture too: Ipsos found 40% of Britons think police should give equal priority to the right to protest and the safety of hotel residents.

ConclusionImproving the sense of safety needs two things at once: fast and visible handling of serious cases, including deportation, and transparent, regularly published local data. Without the second, real risk and inflated fear cannot be told apart.
